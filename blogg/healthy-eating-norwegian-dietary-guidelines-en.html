Healthy eating in Norway: the national dietary guidelines explained in plain English

6 November 2026 7 min read Prevention and lifestyle

Moving to Norway often means a new supermarket, new brands and a whole new set of food labels – many of them in a language you may not read yet. If you have wondered what a "healthy" Norwegian diet is supposed to look like, you are not alone. Here are the official national dietary guidelines, called kostråd, translated into something you can use at your next shop.

What are the kostråd?

The Norwegian Directorate of Health (Helsedirektoratet) publishes the national dietary guidelines for the population. The latest version was updated in August 2024. The advice is based on research and has one main message: get more of your food from plants, and cut back on foods that are salty, sugary or heavily processed.

The Norwegian Institute of Public Health (Folkehelseinstituttet, FHI) reports in its public health report that most Norwegians are some way from the guidelines. Only 14 per cent of adults eat as much fruit, berries and vegetables as recommended. So if you find it hard to hit the targets, you are in good company – and you do not have to change everything at once.

The numbers behind the advice

These are the main recommendations for adults, as the Directorate of Health words them:

  • Fruit, berries and vegetables: at least 5 portions a day, ideally 8. One portion is about 100 grams. Dried fruit and potatoes do not count.
  • Wholegrain: wholegrain products in at least two meals a day, adding up to at least 90 grams of wholegrain daily.
  • Fish and seafood: 300–450 grams a week, of which at least 200 grams should be oily fish. That is roughly two to three dinners.
  • Legumes (beans, lentils and peas): at least one dinner a week.
  • Nuts: 20–30 grams of unsalted nuts a day – about a small handful.
  • Red meat: no more than about 350 grams a week (cooked weight), and as little processed meat as possible, such as cold cuts, bacon and sausages.
  • Drinks: water is the first choice. Sugary drinks and alcohol should be kept low.

According to FHI, Norwegians on average eat more than double the recommended amount of red meat, and intake of salt and added sugar is above the recommended level. On the other hand, most of us eat too little fish and wholegrain.

The plate model: an easy way to eyeball it

You do not need kitchen scales. Helsenorge, the official Norwegian health portal, describes a simple plate model:

  • Half the plate is vegetables, fruit or berries.
  • A quarter is carbohydrates – wholegrain bread, oats, potatoes or wholegrain pasta.
  • A quarter is protein-rich food such as fish, eggs, legumes, meat or dairy.

Helsenorge also suggests using oil or soft margarine instead of butter, choosing lean products, and boiling or steaming more often than frying.

Small changes that add up

Pick one small change a week – for example swapping one meat dinner for fish or beans, or adding a vegetable to lunch. Habits you keep matter more than big plans you drop after two weeks.

What about salt and sugar?

This is where most of us are above the recommendation. According to FHI, the recommended maximum for salt is 5.75 grams a day, while estimated intake is around 11 grams for men and 7.7 grams for women. Added sugar makes up about 12 per cent of energy intake, against a recommended maximum of 10 per cent. Much of the salt does not come from the salt shaker but from bread, cold cuts, ready meals and processed products. Cooking more from scratch and choosing products low in salt and sugar is therefore often more effective than cutting back on what you add at the table.

Practical tips for a busy week in Norway

  • Use the freezer: frozen vegetables and berries are as good a choice as fresh, and they keep for months.
  • Plan three or four dinners ahead. It makes shopping easier and helps you fit in fish and vegetables.
  • Make wholegrain your default: look for grovt brød (coarse wholegrain bread) and havregryn (rolled oats).
  • Look for the Keyhole label (Nøkkelhullet). It is a quick way to find products with less salt, sugar and saturated fat.
  • Keep water handy at work and at home, so it is easier to skip soft drinks and squash.

When should you talk to your GP?

The dietary guidelines are meant for healthy adults. In Norway, your regular GP (fastlege) is the natural first contact. You should get in touch if you:

  • lose weight without trying to, or notice lasting changes in your appetite
  • have recurring discomfort after meals, pain or trouble swallowing
  • suspect you do not tolerate certain foods
  • have a known medical condition or take regular medication and are considering big changes to your diet
  • are pregnant or planning a pregnancy and wonder what suits you

For sudden, severe symptoms such as chest pain or difficulty breathing, always call 113. Outside GP opening hours you can call the out-of-hours service (legevakt) on 116 117. Diet is also a natural part of an annual health check with your GP, and it ties in closely with the health numbers worth knowing and staying active day to day.
