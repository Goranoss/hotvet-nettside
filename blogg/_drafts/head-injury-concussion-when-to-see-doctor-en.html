Hit your head? When to watch at home, call legevakt or call 113 in Drammen

16 November 2026 6 min read Who to call

Your first Norwegian winter can be a surprise: Drammen's pavements turn to ice almost overnight, and a slip on the hills up to Bragernes is easy. Add contact sports, cycling and small children who climb everything, and a knock to the head is something most families meet sooner or later. Most of the time it is harmless – but it helps to know what to watch for, and who to call in the Norwegian system if you are worried.

First, who does what in Norway?

If you are new to Norway, the three numbers and services below are worth knowing before anything happens:

  • Your fastlege (GP) is your regular doctor. Everyone registered in Norway has the right to one. The GP handles follow-up, sick notes and questions that can wait until opening hours.
  • Legevakt – 116 117 is the out-of-hours urgent care service, open evenings, nights and weekends. In Norway you are expected to call first rather than just turning up. The nurse on the phone can give advice and tell you whether to come in. We explain more in What Drammen legevakt does – and doesn't do.
  • 113 is the medical emergency number – the equivalent of 999 or 911 for ambulance. The operators speak English.

Why the first 24 hours matter

Most bumps to the head cause nothing more than a lump, a headache and a fright. But the Norwegian medical information service NHI explains that a mild head injury can sometimes be followed by a problem that develops gradually, so regular checks during the first day are important. In practice: the person who hit their head should not be alone for the first 24 hours. NHI also advises that anyone who lost consciousness, even briefly, should be assessed by a doctor, who decides whether further tests such as a CT scan are needed.

Common symptoms after a knock to the head

  • a moderate headache
  • dizziness or feeling unsteady
  • nausea
  • brief confusion, or not remembering the moment itself
  • poor concentration and unusual tiredness
  • feeling more irritable, low or anxious than usual

According to NHI, symptoms like these usually settle on their own within a few days to a few weeks. A doctor is the right person to judge what is causing them.

Call 113 immediately if

Someone stays unconscious, becomes harder to wake or more confused, has a seizure, cannot move an arm or leg, has slurred speech or unequal pupils, or might have injured their neck. NHI reminds us to always suspect a neck injury after a serious blow to the head – avoid moving the person unless necessary. If in doubt, call.

When to call legevakt (116 117)

Out of hours, call 116 117 if you or the person who was hurt develops any of these. Both NHI and the patient leaflet on head injuries from Helse Bergen (a Norwegian public hospital trust) list similar warning signs:

  • a headache that gets worse or is severe
  • repeated nausea or vomiting
  • problems with balance or vision
  • increasing irritability, confusion or unusual sleepiness
  • difficulty waking up or answering normally

On blood thinners, or older?

Then the bar for calling should be lower. A 2024 commentary in the Journal of the Norwegian Medical Association notes that blood-thinning treatment means more people who hit their head end up with bleeding inside the skull, and that falls are one of the biggest health burdens for people over 75 in Norway. If you take blood thinners and have hit your head, call legevakt or your GP the same day – even if you feel fine.

Watching at home: practical tips

  • Stay with them. Helse Bergen recommends that a relative keeps an eye on the injured person for the first day, especially children.
  • Check they wake normally. If they sleep, wake them and make sure they respond as usual. Advice on how often varies – NHI mentions once or twice during the first night if no CT scan was taken, while Helse Bergen suggests more frequent checks in the first hours. Follow what the doctor or legevakt tells you.
  • Skip alcohol until you feel back to normal.
  • Cool a lump with something cold wrapped in a towel.

The days after: not total rest, not full speed

NHI's advice after concussion is that complete rest for more than two days is not recommended, as it may make symptoms last longer. After a couple of days, build up gradually: short walks and light tasks, then work or school, then exercise. Short periods of screen time are fine as long as they don't make you feel worse. Avoid contact sports while you still have symptoms.

On work: Norway lets employees self-certify short absences (egenmelding), and your GP can issue a sick note (sykmelding). The Norwegian Directorate of Health's guidance for doctors recommends at least two days off after a concussion, with anything longer based on symptoms – and a part-time (graded) sick note is often used to ease you back in.

When to book your GP

  • if you need a sick note or follow-up
  • if headache, dizziness, poor concentration or tiredness are still bothering you after about four weeks
  • if you are unsure when it is safe to return to sport

Children who bump their heads

Young children bump their heads all the time, and nearly always it is fine. The same warning signs apply, but call sooner – especially if your child is unusually floppy, vomits more than once, won't play as normal or can't be comforted. Helse Bergen notes that children who are well can usually go back to barnehage (nursery) or school the next day. For night-time fever worries, see child with a fever in the night.

Remember

You know yourself and your family best. If something seems "not right" after a knock to the head, it is always OK to call 116 117 – or 113 if you are frightened it is serious.
